1.1.1  About VNUK

VNUK (Vietnam-UK Institute for Research and Training) is an international public higher education institution under the University of Danang, established and operating under Decision No. 5555/QD-BGDDT dated November 22, 2013. According to the official description, VNUK was built to realize the cooperation policy of the Government of Vietnam and the UK in the direction of developing an international university in Da Nang, and VNUK is the foundation/premise for the next development roadmap.

  1.  Vision & Mission:

VNUK positions its vision towards world class and emphasizes a "creative and human-centered" learning environment, while also aiming to contribute to the quality of research and training according to international standards.

  1. Core Value:

The three core values ​​announced by VNUK are: Innovation - Empathy - Perseverance. VNUK emphasizes that these values ​​are "permeated in every activity, every experience and every goal" of the organization.

1.1.2  About VNUK’s CSE Major (The Micro Focus)

  1. Program Identity: 

In the admission/target documents, the Computer Science major is recorded with the admission registration code 7480101–CSE (Computer Science & Engineering).

  1. Scale:

According to public admission information on VNUK's official website, the Computer Science & Engineering major (major code 7480101) has annual enrollment quotas announced in the school's major/major table for recent enrollment periods.

Besides, according to internal data provided by the CSE department (as of December 2024), the program is currently training about 220 students, with an average enrollment size of approximately 70–80 students per year.

This scale shows that CSE is a program with a relatively concentrated student population, in which academic and administrative processes (especially course registration, project/thesis registration) have a direct and clear influence on the experience of the majority of students in the department.

  1. Curriculum Context: 

The Computer Science (CSE) program at VNUK is designed with an in-depth and updated technical orientation, with a learning path ranging from foundational modules such as Introduction to Computer Science, Programming, Data Structures, to advanced and highly applicable modules such as Machine Learning, Artificial Intelligence, Internet of Things (IoT) and project modules.

The outstanding feature of the program is the large proportion of subjects and project-based learning activities, requiring students to regularly register for courses, register for projects, work with instructors and comply with strict academic and administrative timelines.

1.2.2  Actual Experience at the CSE Faculty at VNUK (Reality)

  1. Online Experience

Strengths
At the CSE faculty, there are currently several platforms that support learning, such as:

  • Canvas system for learning activities and assignment submission.
  • Zalo groups for quick information exchange.
  • Official fanpage for updates and announcements, with information also communicated via email.
  • DaoTao website for credit updates and course registration.

Weaknesses
However, these systems operate in a fragmented manner. Although platforms are available, students tend to use them infrequently and instead prioritize direct communication (for example, during interviews, some students reported not having accessed Canvas for nearly two years).

There is a lack of integration within the CSE faculty, and many processes remain slow and insufficiently standardized. For instance, in the graduation project process, students must create their own project formats and only receive general suggestions from supervisors, rather than being provided with a standardized roadmap or detailed brief outlining specific sections and requirements, as seen in other faculties such as IBM and DBM.

As a result, students often have to independently compile information from multiple sources, leading to the following issues:

  • A high likelihood of missing important information (such as internship project registration or notifications related to graduation documentation).
  • Time-consuming information searching and delays due to waiting periods in approval processes.
  1. Offline Experience

Strengths
 In terms of offline experience, VNUK offers many positive activities, including:

  • Learning and creative spaces (Maker Space), along with numerous workshops where students can participate, exchange ideas, and discuss topics such as AI, digital transformation, and TikTok.
  • Mental health support programs, such as Mental Health Week.
  • Diverse academic and extracurricular activities, such as field trips integrated into the curriculum, providing students with opportunities to gain real-world exposure, exchange perspectives, and develop a clearer and more comprehensive understanding of enterprises and career pathways.

 Weaknesses
 However, this experience still presents several limitations:

Although there have been many improvements in physical facilities, for the CSE faculty where programs place high demands on digital skills and rapidly evolving technologies—the computer labs, in terms of functions and tools, are not yet fully equipped to comprehensively support students during their learning process. Students are generally only able to use basic tools for foundational courses (such as applied computer courses), while for more advanced subjects, students are required to prepare their own tools and software.

The level of academic support is not consistent among lecturers. While some lecturers provide close guidance and strong student support, others offer more limited assistance. As a result, many students are required to proactively “manage on their own” during critical stages, independently seeking additional resources and solutions. Consequently, the learning experience remains highly individualized and heavily dependent on students’ personal initiative.

Academic guidance processes have not been standardized. Specifically, during the registration process and throughout the thesis/project period, students typically meet lecturers directly to ask questions and receive guidance. Support is mainly provided through verbal explanations and general suggestions, rather than through clearly defined briefs or roadmaps that help students visualize the overall structure and expectations. The supporting documents provided are also perceived by students as relatively outdated and not fully updated to reflect current developments.

Students are required to proactively “manage on their own” during several key stages. For example, during the thesis/project phase, students must independently develop their own outline and ideas without a predefined brief or roadmap. Similarly, during the graduation stage, students are responsible for contacting relevant parties, coordinating communication, and preparing required documentation on their own.

1.3  Context of Computer Science Faculty

  1. Hierarchy

VNUK is organised according to a “leadership – advisory – operational – academic” model. At the institutional level, the Rector and Vice Rectors are responsible for the overall management and strategic direction of the Institute. The Academic and Scientific Council plays an advisory role in matters related to education, research, collaboration, and quality assurance. Supporting these bodies are various functional departments, which ensure the smooth operation of the Institute. At the academic level, faculties are responsible for organising teaching and research activities.

Within this structure, the Computer Science (CSE) programme is positioned under the Faculty of Information Technology and Communication, which belongs to the broader group of science and technology faculties. The CSE programme was formally established in the 2023–2024 academic period. Before that, it developed from a smaller academic unit  within the Faculty of Science and Technology, and was later separated into a distinct programme as student demand and academic scope expanded.

As a relatively young and still developing programme, CSE remains small in scale compared to more established faculties. The internal structure of the programme includes key academic roles such as a programme head, deputy head, academic affairs officer, and lecturers. Each role contributes to maintaining academic quality and supporting programme delivery.

At the faculty level, the Faculty of Information Technology and Communication manages the overall operation of its programmes, including CSE. The faculty is responsible for managing training programmes, coordinating course delivery, assigning teaching responsibilities, and ensuring academic quality and disciplinary standards. Lecturers under the faculty carry out teaching, assessment, research, and supervision of graduation projects.

  1. Activities:

Based on the interview with a Computer Science lecturer, the activities of the CSE faculty cover a range of academic and student-related functions. These activities are primarily centred on teaching, research, and graduation projects, while other areas such as student engagement and industry collaboration are still in the process of development.

Teaching and learning activities remain the core and strongest focus of the CSE faculty. The faculty places significant emphasis on instructional quality, curriculum delivery, and academic outcomes. Lecturers are actively involved in course design, classroom teaching, and assessment processes. According to the interview, teaching quality and graduation projects together account for approximately 70% of the faculty’s academic focus, indicating that this area is relatively well-developed and clearly prioritised.

In terms of research and graduation projects, the faculty also demonstrates strong performance. Academic research and final-year projects are treated as key responsibilities, with lecturers closely supervising student work throughout the process. This reflects the faculty’s orientation toward academic depth and research-based learning, particularly for upper-year students.

Regarding student support and academic life, the faculty is expected to assist students with learning difficulties, project guidance, and academic progression. In practice, however, student support activities remain limited in scale. Most student-related matters are handled through coordination with the Student Affairs Office (CTSV). Information is typically transferred from CTSV to the faculty and then redistributed to relevant lecturers, which can reduce the speed and directness of support.

Student activities and engagement, including extracurricular events, community building, and soft-skill development, are not yet a major focus of the CSE faculty. Due to the relatively small size of the programme and its recent establishment, such activities remain limited and are not systematically organised at the faculty level.

With respect to industry connection and external engagement, these activities are recognised as important but are still developing. While some collaboration exists through projects and internships, industry engagement is not yet systematic and remains relatively limited in visibility from the student perspective.

The impact of institutional integration has also influenced faculty operations. Since the integration of VNUK into the University of Da Nang system and the VN-UK university framework, faculty-level activities have become more procedural. Additional administrative layers have increased coordination requirements, affecting the speed of decision-making and implementation.

Finally, in terms of organisational coordination, the faculty currently has one designated staff member responsible for student affairs coordination. Student-related issues are first received through student affairs coordination , then routed to the faculty, and finally transferred to relevant academic or administrative focal points. While this structure ensures alignment with institutional procedures, it also increases dependency on cross-unit coordination and may reduce operational flexibility.

  1. Performance:

According to the interviews with 15 Computer Science students, the overall performance of the CSE faculty presents a combination of strengths and areas for improvement across teaching quality, academic support, and operational processes. In terms of teaching quality and consistency, students generally acknowledge the dedication of lecturers and their willingness to support learning.

Regarding academic support and responsiveness, students appreciate the approachability of lecturers and academic advisors, but report inconsistent response times. In several cases, students experienced delays of four to five days when seeking academic guidance, highlighting the need for more timely and structured support, especially during critical academic periods.

A frequently mentioned issue relates to the clarity of academic roadmaps and key academic procedures, such as course registration and thesis or graduation project enrollment. Many students described these processes as unclear and fragmented, with limited step-by-step guidance and rigid procedures, which often caused confusion and uncertainty throughout their academic journey.

In addition, students raised concerns about the stability of digital systems and learning infrastructure, including Wi-Fi connectivity, LMS platforms, course registration websites, and laboratory facilities. Technical issues, outdated equipment, and system overload during peak periods were perceived as factors that negatively affected learning efficiency and overall experience.

From a social perspective, some students noted a limited sense of community and belonging within the CSE program, with few structured opportunities to connect with senior students or peers. Furthermore, in terms of career readiness and industry connection, students expressed a desire for stronger networking opportunities, clearer career guidance, and more engagement with alumni or industry professionals, as practical exposure was seen to depend largely on individual courses or lecturers. Despite these challenges, overall satisfaction remains relatively positive, with most students rating their experience between 7 and 9 out of 10.

2.2  CX Vision

While a significant 83% of VNUk lecturers are reported to possess high expertise, supportiveness, and enthusiasm, a concerning 17% are described as "thờ ơ" (indifferent) and inactive. Even with supportive lecturers, 83% of interviewees noted that students must be extremely proactive to receive assistance. This indicates that effective help is currently only accessible to students who are already proactive, aware of their needs, and know precisely whom to approach.

The current system only rewards proactive students, limiting the potential of others. However, the most transformative moments for students occur when they find an inspiring, lifelong mentor who guides their academic and career paths (as seen in high-performing students like Hieu (K20), Khai (K23), and Dat (K22)). Finding such an inspirational teacher can significantly improve the university performance of CSE students.

Aligning with VNUK's core value of Empathy (part of "Innovation – Compassion – Diligence"), we propose a change for CX Vision:

Empowering the next generation of tech leaders through a proactive support system that leaves no talent behind.

To achieve this, the support system must be comprehensive, addressing the three specific layers of friction identified in the student journey. The student experience is hampered by unreliable Tech Support, reactive Admin Support, and an inaccessible Teacher & Mentorship system. Students face high anxiety from system crashes (e.g., during registration) and manual administrative processes leading to crises (e.g., nearly missing graduation), while mentorship has a "Proactivity Gap" where help is only available "if you dare to ask." The proposed vision is a shift to a proactively supportive environment through: Proactive Tech (Auto-Save, 'Traffic Light Status' for trust); Proactive Admin (automation for timely alerts, like "IELTS expiring"); and Proactive Mentorship (data-driven outreach by faculty to struggling students, shifting the initiation burden from the student to the institution).

This vision creates a strategic mandate to shift VNUK CSE from a "survival of the fittest" culture (where students "swim alone") to a "thrive together" ecosystem. It operationalizes VNUK’s core value of Empathy by ensuring that administrative and academic support is not a hidden privilege for the bold, but a standard for all.

"Leaving no talent behind" means creating a Safety Net. It acknowledges that a student dropping out because they couldn't navigate a confusing registration portal is a failure of the institution, not the student. Empathy in CX means designing processes for the anxious student, not just the confident one. By removing the barrier of "having to ask," we ensure that all students, not just the ones with good relationships, have equal access to the resources they need to become "Tech Leaders." This gives every student the preparation they need to thrive in the 100% employment market, fulfilling the promise of the vision.

2.3 CX 3-year Strategy

Year 1: Stabilization – Building the "Safety Net"

The immediate priority is to operationalize the "leaves no talent behind" promise by fixing the foundational breaks in the student journey. Currently, the journey is "reactive," leading to an 8.11% dropout rate and anxiety around basic processes like course registration and graduation filing. The goal for Year 1 is to deploy a proactive early-warning system, not just for grades, but for administrative milestones. By shifting from "waiting for students to ask" to "alerting students before they fail," the faculty will eliminate the "administrative anxiety" that currently plagues the freshman and senior experience. Success here means stabilizing retention and ensuring no student drops out due to process confusion.

Year 2: Integration – The "Information Ecosystem"

Once the safety net is in place, the focus shifts to "empowering" students through a unified information ecosystem. Interview data reveals that students currently rely on fragmented sources (friends, scattered emails, old Facebook posts) to navigate their academic path. Year 2 focuses on centralizing this knowledge into a Single Source of Truth that is transparent and accessible 24/7. This removes the "self-swim" friction, allowing students to focus their energy on becoming "tech leaders" (coding, research, projects) rather than navigating bureaucracy. The goal is to move from a manual-run faculty to a tech-enabled ecosystem where information flows seamlessly to the student.

Year 3: Acceleration – The "Leadership Loop"

The final phase leverages the faculty’s strongest asset—its 100% employment rate—to fulfill the "tech leader" promise. With the internal friction removed, the strategy focuses on external connection. This year formalizes the loop between the "Next Gen" (current students) and the "Current Leaders" (Alumni). By transforming successful alumni from passive graduates into active mentors and industry bridges, the faculty creates a self-sustaining culture of guidance. This ensures that "leaving no talent behind" becomes a community-driven value, where seniors and alumni actively pull juniors up the ladder, bridging the gap between academic theory and industry reality.

3.1.2  The Reality Experience Data (X-Data)

  1. The Student Voice

To gain a deeper understanding of the underlying challenges within the CSE student journey, the study conducted in-depth conversations with 15 CSE students and 5 faculty members.. The research team chose a direct approach through personal messages and face-to-face meetings instead of online surveys. This approach helps us capture hidden "pains" that students find difficult to express in writing, while observing frustration and real emotions when they face administrative barrierscore factors that quantitative surveys often ignore.

Through a series of semi-structured interviews and ethnographic observations, this study has systematically codified the primary friction points encountered by CSE students. By triangulating qualitative narratives with experience metrics, the following quantitative insights emerge, highlighting the emotional and functional gaps within the current student journey.

The most prominent area of concern is the Administrative Registration System, which functions as a "peak frustration" touchpoint. Data indicates that 85% of students identify the course and thesis registration process as the most stressful experience in their academic lifecycle. This stress is largely driven by technical instability, with 72% of respondents reporting that the student portal consistently fails or experiences significant latency during the critical first 30 minutes of the registration window. Furthermore, a lack of procedural clarity forces 60% of students to rely on peer-to-peer networks for guidance, as official institutional instructions are perceived as too generic to be actionable.

Regarding Infrastructure and Curriculum Alignment, the data reveals a motivational gap during the early stages of the program. Approximately 55% of students express disengagement with general education courses, citing a lack of perceived relevance to their future careers in technology. This sentiment is compounded by physical resource constraints; 50% of students report that campus infrastructure, specifically Wi-Fi stability and high-performance computing hardware, does not fully meet the rigorous demands of advanced, specialized projects.

In contrast, Faculty Interaction remains a core strength of the CSE experience. An overwhelming 95% of students highly commend the academic expertise and dedication of [a][b]their lecturers. However, a significant "Mentorship Friction" exists, as 40% of students experience delays in receiving feedback. This lag is attributed to a high supervisor-to-student ratio, where faculty members are burdened by overextended supervision loads, hindering their ability to provide the high-touch support that engineering students require.

In summary, the quantification of these pain points indicates that registration-related hurdles account for approximately 85% of the total perceived friction across the CSE student journey. Within this, thesis topic registration represents the largest source of friction (around 40%), driven by unclear topic approval criteria, limited supervisor availability, and highly manual registration and communication processes.   1. Faculty Perspective

Faculty interviews ( with Dean: Lê Đình Dũng) provide critical insight into the institutional and operational context shaping the CSE student experience. As a recently established faculty upgraded from a smaller academic unit, the CSE faculty is operating under transitional conditions marked by limited human resources and evolving organisational structures. Interview data consistently indicate a heavy reliance on manual administrative processes and fragmented information systems. These conditions increase the risk of operational errors and constrain the faculty’s ability to deliver coherent, end-to-end academic support. Furthermore, faculty members acknowledged a growing misalignment between students’ expectations for applied, industry-oriented learning and the faculty’s predominantly research-oriented academic framework.

The capstone project process was identified as a primary point of friction affecting both students and academic staff. Faculty members reported that supervision requests are currently managed through informal, email-based communication initiated by students. This approach has resulted in uneven supervision loads, with certain lecturers receiving a disproportionate number of requests while others receive none. Faculty respondents also highlighted the absence of formal mechanisms to support topic–supervisor matching and to mediate rejection or redirection decisions. Despite these challenges, faculty members expressed a flexible stance toward capstone formats, emphasising that the quality of the final defense outcome is prioritised over rigid distinctions between research-based and applied projects. The lack of a transparent and centralised allocation process therefore represents a significant experiential gap within the capstone journey.

Beyond academic processes, faculty interviews revealed substantial weaknesses in administrative and student service operations. In many cases, important announcements are delivered solely through individual emails. For example, the most recent graduation assessment announcement was issued in 2022, while the official procedures and forms were last updated in 2021. Although certain adjustments have been made, this information has not been consistently updated on a centralized platform, creating risks that students may miss emails, overlook critical information, or rely on outdated documents.

This situation results in several significant negative impacts on the student experience:

  • Students find it difficult to locate the correct information for each stage and specific academic need.
  • Repeated one-on-one email communication increases workload and pressure for both students and administrative staff.
  • There is no “single source of truth” that allows students to proactively track, prepare, and manage their academic processes.

Example: The most recent graduation assessment announcement was released in 2022.

In particular, the procedures and official forms were most recently updated in 2021, indicating a lack of timely and consistent updates.

Although some updates were made, they were communicated only through individual emails and were not published on a centralized platform, which increases the risk of email delivery issues or students missing important information.

3.4.2  Overall Solution Direction

To address this core issue, it is necessary to centralize and standardize all academic-related information on a single official platform, specifically the university’s academic website (daotao.vnuk.udn.vn). This platform should function as a central academic information hub, where each major academic task is clearly separated into individual tabs or sections, standardized in content, and continuously updated.

Instead of requiring students to search for information across multiple fragmented channels, a centralized academic system enables students to access information more easily, track their progress more effectively, and remain proactive throughout their academic journey and graduation preparation. At the same time, this approach significantly reduces the administrative workload associated with manual email handling and minimizes errors in academic communication.

In addition, the system should incorporate a major long-term guidance component, namely a “Student Handbook”, which provides a comprehensive and unified overview of key academic procedures. This component serves as a foundational reference to support students in navigating essential academic milestones.

3.4.3  Proposed Solution Structure by Tasks and Sections

In addition to the core academic tasks, a major overarching section titled “Student Handbook” is introduced and further divided into smaller, task-specific sections as outlined below.

Tab 1: Course Registration / Credit Registration

This section provides a standardized course display layout, clearly distinguishing between compulsory courses and elective courses. The system integrates filtering functions by course type and continuously updates the academic roadmap by cohort and academic year, enabling students to select courses accurately and in alignment with their study plan.

Tab 2: Training Roadmap by Cohort

This section standardizes the training roadmap for each specific cohort and updates changes annually. Centralizing roadmaps on a single platform helps prevent students from using outdated or incorrect versions, thereby reducing confusion during academic planning.

Tab 3: Thesis Registration

This section offers standardized procedures from the early stages, including formatting guidelines, roadmaps, checklists, and thesis timelines. All information is continuously updated on the system, allowing students to gain a clear understanding of expectations and remain proactive throughout the thesis implementation process.

Tab 4: Graduation Registration

This section provides an official graduation handbook detailing graduation requirements, documentation, conditions, and timelines by cohort. The system integrates standardized forms that allow students to report issues related to academic records or certificate information, replacing manual one-on-one email communication. This approach helps reduce response overload and minimizes information errors.

5.1 Organization: Governance & Accountability

Based on the interview with Thầy Dũng, the root cause of high-risk incidents (like the near-miss graduation case) is Data Silos. The student journey is fractured between Admissions (Input), Faculty (Processing), and Training Dept (Output), leaving the Faculty blind to critical deadlines (e.g., IELTS expiry).

 Recommendation 1: Establish the "Student Success Taskforce" (Governance)

To deliver Solutions 5 & 7 (Graduation Handbook & Updated Roadmaps), the faculty cannot work in isolation. The Dean must lead a taskforce with the Training Department to agree on a "Master Data Protocol."

Define who owns the framework for student roadmaps. Currently, roadmaps change yearly, causing confusion. The Faculty must have the authority to publish the definitive roadmap for each cohort on the Central Hub (Solution 8).

Work together to sync data definitions (e.g., confirming graduation criteria 6 months in advance) and proactively review "Red Light" cases, students at risk of dropping out or missing credentials, before deadlines hit.

Negotiate "Read-Only" access to the central training database. As noted by Dean Dũng, the faculty currently lacks control over end-to-end data (e.g., IELTS expiry). The Taskforce ensures that the Hub displays real-time status, not outdated Excel copies.

Recommendations 2: Enforce the "No Form, No Service" Policy (Accountability)

To deliver Solution 4 (Centralized Correction Request Form) and stop the "email flood", the Dean must issue a strict directive. Administrative staff are authorized to ignore unstructured emails regarding grade errors or graduation checks. All requests must go through the Standardized Google form (Solution 4). This forces the behavioral change required to clean up the data.

5.2 Technology Ecosystem: The "Centralized Hub" Strategy

To establish daotao.vnuk.udn.vn as the authoritative "Single Source of Truth," eliminating the fragmentation of information across emails and PDF files.

1. The Core Platform: daotao.vnuk.udn.vn Instead of building risky new software, the strategy leverages the existing university domain as the central repository. This page must be restructured from a static news feed into a functional dashboard with four standardized tabs:

• Tab 2: Course Registration: clearly distinguishes "Compulsory" vs. "Elective" modules to prevent the confusion identified in the problem brief.

• Tab 3: Capstone Marketplace: Acts as the "Exchange Floor" where lecturers update their available slots and research directions, replacing the manual email spam,.

• Tab 4: Graduation & Admin: A repository for the "Graduation Handbook" and the centralized "Correction Request Form",.

2. IT Support Requirement: "Content Currency" Agreement

Currently The Faculty lacks the permissions to modify the core website structure ndependently. The Faculty must negotiate a Service Level Agreement (SLA) with the central IT team.

IT is not required to build complex code. Their mandate is "Continuous Updating". They must ensure that when the Faculty Admin sends a new roadmap or deadline, it is uploaded to the page within 24 hours. Information friction occurs not because the tech doesn't exist, but because the information on it is outdated (e.g., 2022 announcements still visible). Constant updates ensure the page remains relevant.

5.3 Cultural Change: The "Redirect & Educate" Approach

To shift student behavior from "Passive Reliance" (waiting for emails/asking privately) to "Proactive Self-Service" (checking the Hub).

  • Impact 1: The "Embedded Link" Touchpoint Strategy

 To break the habit of emailing admin staff for basic information, we will change the design of every Admin Touchpoint (emails, auto-replies): Every email sent by the Faculty (Admin/Student Affairs) must include a standardized footer/signature containing deep links to the daotao page.This serves as a constant "Nudge", training students that the answer lies on the website, not in the inbox. It operationalizes the "Knowledge Base" concept suggested by Thầy Dũng to reduce admin overload.

  •  Impact 2: Shift from "Asking" to "Searching" (Proactivity)

Students feel they are "swimming alone" (tự bơi) because they expect information to be pushed to them personally, and when it isn't, they panic.

Promoting New Culture is  "Proactive Independence."  Administrative staff will prioritize requests submitted via the Centralized Forms (on the Hub) over unstructured emails. Have students be aware of the message of  "The school guarantees the information is available and correct on the Hub. It is your responsibility to check it." This empowers students to take control of their journey while relieving the emotional burden on staff to "rescue" students manually.
